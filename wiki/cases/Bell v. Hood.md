# Bell v. Hood

## Citation and Court
327 U.S. 678 (1946), Supreme Court of the United States

## Facts
Bell sued FBI agents for allegedly violating his Fourth and Fifth Amendment rights by conducting an unlawful search and seizure. The district court dismissed for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on the ground that the complaint failed to state a cause of action, since no explicit federal statute created a damages remedy for constitutional violations by federal officers.

## Issue
Whether a federal court has subject matter jurisdiction over a claim that arises under the Constitution when the plaintiff may ultimately fail to state a valid cause of action or the claim is ultimately without merit.

## Holding
Federal question jurisdiction exists whenever a complaint presents a claim arising under the Constitution or federal law; the merits of the claim — whether it actually states a cause of action or will succeed — are a separate inquiry from the jurisdictional question.

## Rule / Doctrine
A case "arises under" federal law for jurisdictional purposes if the plaintiff's right to relief depends on the construction or application of the Constitution or federal law. The court's jurisdiction does not depend on whether the plaintiff will prevail; dismissal for failure to state a claim is on the merits, not for lack of jurisdiction, unless the claim is "wholly insubstantial and frivolous."

## Significance
Bell v. Hood establishes the fundamental distinction between subject matter jurisdiction and the merits of a claim. It is a foundational case for federal question jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 and is regularly cited for the principle that a court must not conflate jurisdictional dismissal with merits dismissal.
