# Bethel v. New York City Transit Authority

## Citation and Court
Bethel v. New York City Transit Authority, 92 N.Y.2d 348 (N.Y. 1998)

## Facts
Plaintiff Bethel, a wheelchair user, was a passenger on a New York City Transit Authority bus. A special accessibility seat designed for disabled passengers collapsed beneath him, causing injury. At trial, the court instructed the jury using the traditional common carrier standard, which required the Transit Authority to exercise the "highest degree of care" consistent with the practical operation of its business. The Transit Authority argued that ordinary negligence was the appropriate standard.

## Issue
Should common carriers be held to a heightened standard of the "highest degree of care," or does ordinary reasonable care govern their obligations to passengers?

## Holding
The New York Court of Appeals abolished the heightened common carrier standard and held that common carriers owe their passengers only the duty of reasonable care under all the circumstances — the same standard as any other defendant in negligence.

## Rule / Doctrine
Common carriers are no longer held to a special heightened standard of the "utmost" or "highest" degree of care. All negligence cases — including those involving common carriers — are governed by the single reasonable person standard: what would a reasonably prudent person do under the circumstances? The circumstances still include the nature of the relationship (carrier and passenger) and the degree of risk involved, but these are factors in the negligence analysis rather than triggers for a separate, more demanding standard.

## Significance
Bethel v. New York City Transit Authority is a significant modernization of the duty of care in New York, eliminating a categorical exception that dated from the era of horse-drawn carriages and early railroads. The court reasoned that the special standard had developed when common carriers were unusually dangerous, but modern safety regulations and changed conditions made a categorical distinction unjustified. The case illustrates how courts periodically revisit categorical duty rules in light of changed circumstances and the trend toward a unified negligence standard.
