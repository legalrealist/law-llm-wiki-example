# American Needle, Inc. v. NFL

**Citation:** 560 U.S. 183 (Supreme Court, 2010)

## Facts
The NFL's 32 teams collectively formed a licensing entity (NFL Properties) to license their intellectual property — team names, logos, and trademarks — to manufacturers on an exclusive basis. American Needle had previously held a non-exclusive license to make NFL-branded headwear but lost it when the NFL granted an exclusive license to Reebok. American Needle sued under Sherman Act § 1, alleging a conspiracy in restraint of trade. The NFL argued it was a single entity incapable of conspiring with itself.

## Issue
Whether the NFL and its 32 member teams constitute a single entity for purposes of Sherman Act § 1 such that their joint licensing decisions are immune from § 1 scrutiny.

## Holding
No. The Supreme Court held unanimously that the NFL teams are separate economic actors with separate economic interests and that their collective licensing decisions constitute concerted action subject to § 1 analysis. The case was remanded for rule of reason analysis.

## Rule
The single-entity defense under § 1 turns on whether the alleged conspirators have a "unity of interest" — whether they are truly a single enterprise pursuing a single set of economic interests. Separate entities with separate economic stakes in competition with one another are capable of conspiring even when they cooperate through a common entity. The NFL teams compete against each other for fans, players, coaches, and merchandise revenue, and thus retain separate interests.

## Significance
American Needle is the key case for the single-entity defense in antitrust law and is essential reading alongside Copperweld Corp. v. Independence Tube Corp. (holding that a parent and wholly owned subsidiary are a single entity). The case sets the test for when a joint venture or trade association is so integrated that it acts as one entity versus when it remains a collection of independent actors. It is also significant for understanding how sports leagues straddle the line — requiring horizontal cooperation to function while remaining subject to antitrust scrutiny.
