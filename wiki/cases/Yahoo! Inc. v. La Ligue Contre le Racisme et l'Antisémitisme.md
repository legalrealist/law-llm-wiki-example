# Yahoo! Inc. v. La Ligue Contre le Racisme et l'Antisémitisme

## Citation and Court
169 F. Supp. 2d 1181 (N.D. Cal. 2001), rev'd, 433 F.3d 1199 (9th Cir. 2006) (en banc)

## Facts
A French court ordered Yahoo! to block French users' access to Nazi memorabilia auctions on its website, under French law prohibiting Holocaust denial and the display of objects promoting Nazi ideology. Yahoo! complied in France but sought a declaratory judgment in U.S. federal court that the French court's order was unenforceable in the United States as contrary to the First Amendment. The district court found personal jurisdiction over the French defendants and granted Yahoo! declaratory relief. The Ninth Circuit reversed on jurisdictional grounds.

## Issue
Whether U.S. federal courts have personal jurisdiction over French organizations that obtained a French court order directing a U.S. company to restrict content accessible by French users, and whether a U.S. company may obtain a preemptive declaratory judgment that a foreign speech-restrictive order is unenforceable domestically.

## Holding
The Ninth Circuit held, en banc, that the French organizations lacked sufficient contacts with the United States for personal jurisdiction; the court therefore dismissed the case without reaching the merits of First Amendment enforceability.

## Rule / Doctrine
Foreign defendants who obtain court orders in their home country, without additional purposeful availment of U.S. forums, do not necessarily subject themselves to personal jurisdiction in U.S. courts merely because their order affects a U.S. company. The case also raises the broader public policy principle — discussed extensively by the district court — that U.S. courts should not enforce foreign judgments that are contrary to fundamental First Amendment protections.

## Significance
Yahoo! v. LICRA is one of the most prominent transnational litigation cases involving internet speech, jurisdictional reach of foreign court orders, and the tension between free speech norms and foreign hate speech regulation. It illustrates the public policy exception to the recognition of foreign judgments and the intersection of personal jurisdiction doctrine with cross-border internet governance. The district court's analysis of First Amendment limits on foreign judgment recognition remains widely cited even though the Ninth Circuit avoided the merits.
