# Mapp v. Ohio

**Citation:** 367 U.S. 643 (1961)

## Facts
Cleveland police forcibly entered Dollree Mapp's home without a warrant while searching for a bombing suspect and gambling paraphernalia. They found neither, but discovered obscene materials for which Mapp was convicted under Ohio law. The Supreme Court had previously held in *Wolf v. Colorado* that the Fourth Amendment applies to states but that the exclusionary rule did not.

## Issue
Whether evidence obtained through a search that violates the Fourth Amendment must be excluded from state criminal proceedings.

## Holding
The Supreme Court overruled *Wolf* and held that the exclusionary rule is constitutionally required and applies to state courts through the Fourteenth Amendment. Evidence seized in violation of the Fourth Amendment must be suppressed.

## Rule
Evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment is inadmissible in criminal prosecutions in both federal and state courts. The exclusionary rule is a constitutional remedy for Fourth Amendment violations, not merely a court-made rule of evidence.

## Significance
Mapp is the landmark incorporation case for the exclusionary rule and remains one of the most significant criminal procedure decisions in American history. Together with *Weeks v. United States*, it established the architecture of Fourth Amendment remedies. It is the foundation for study of suppression motions, standing doctrine, and the exceptions to the exclusionary rule.
