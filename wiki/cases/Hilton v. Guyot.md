# Hilton v. Guyot

**Citation:** 159 U.S. 113 (1895)

## Facts
Guyot, a French citizen, obtained a judgment against Hilton and others in a French court after full litigation. Guyot then sued in U.S. federal court to enforce the French judgment. Hilton argued that the French judgment should not be recognized because French courts would not, as a matter of French law, give conclusive effect to a U.S. judgment against a French citizen.

## Issue
Under what circumstances will U.S. federal courts recognize and enforce foreign country judgments?

## Holding
The Supreme Court held that foreign judgments are entitled to recognition under principles of international comity, provided certain conditions are met. However, because France would not give conclusive effect to U.S. judgments, the Court applied a reciprocity requirement and refused full enforcement of the French judgment — instead allowing it to be used as evidence but not as conclusive.

## Rule
Under Hilton's comity-based framework, a foreign judgment is entitled to recognition if: (1) the foreign court had proper jurisdiction; (2) the proceeding was fair; (3) the judgment is final; (4) it does not violate domestic public policy; and (5) reciprocity exists (the foreign country would enforce similar U.S. judgments). The reciprocity requirement has been widely rejected by U.S. states.

## Significance
Hilton v. Guyot is the foundational American case on recognition of foreign judgments and introduces the concept of comity into U.S. private international law. The reciprocity requirement it adopted has been largely rejected in state courts (see Uniform Foreign-Country Money Judgments Recognition Act), making Hilton's practical holding dated but doctrinally essential.
