# Bond v. United States

## Citation and Court
572 U.S. 844 (2014) — Supreme Court of the United States

## Facts
Carol Anne Bond discovered that her husband had an affair with her close friend. In retaliation, Bond placed toxic chemicals on her friend's mailbox, car door, and doorknob, causing the friend a minor chemical burn on her thumb. Federal prosecutors charged Bond under the Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act of 1998. Bond argued that the Act, as applied to her purely local and personal conduct, exceeded Congress's treaty-implementing power and intruded on state police power.

## Issue
Whether the Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act reaches the purely local assault by a jilted spouse using small quantities of toxic chemicals.

## Holding
The Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act does not cover Bond's conduct as a matter of statutory interpretation; the term "chemical weapon" is narrowly construed to avoid serious constitutional questions about the scope of the treaty power versus state sovereignty.

## Rule / Doctrine
Constitutional avoidance canon applied to treaty-implementing legislation: when a broad reading of a federal statute would raise serious constitutional doubts about intrusion on state sovereignty — here, criminalizing what is essentially a local assault — courts construe the statute narrowly to avoid the constitutional question. The statute's text and purpose do not support applying it to the kind of local, non-warfare use of chemicals at issue.

## Significance
Bond avoids deciding the constitutional limits of Congress's power to implement treaties under *Missouri v. Holland* (1920), but the opinions are significant for their extended discussion of federalism and the structural concern that treaty power cannot be used to circumvent limitations on federal legislative power over local conduct.
