# Brady v. United States

**Citation:** 397 U.S. 742 (1970)
**Court:** United States Supreme Court

## Facts

Robert Brady was charged with kidnapping under a federal statute that authorized the death penalty if a jury so recommended. To avoid the risk of a death sentence, Brady pleaded guilty. After his plea was accepted, the Supreme Court held in United States v. Jackson (1968) that the death-penalty provision of the statute was unconstitutional because it penalized the exercise of the right to a jury trial. Brady then sought to withdraw his guilty plea, arguing it was involuntary because it was induced by an unconstitutional threat.

## Issue

Is a guilty plea rendered involuntary when it was entered to avoid the death penalty under a statute that was subsequently held unconstitutional?

## Holding

No. The Supreme Court upheld Brady's guilty plea, holding that a plea entered to avoid the death penalty is not automatically involuntary, even if the statute authorizing the death penalty was later found unconstitutional.

## Rule / Doctrine

The voluntariness of a guilty plea is assessed at the time the plea is entered, based on the totality of the circumstances. A plea is voluntary if made by a defendant who was competent, fully aware of the charges and consequences, and represented by competent counsel. The subsequent unconstitutionality of the statute that motivated the plea does not retroactively render the plea involuntary.

## Significance

Brady v. United States (not to be confused with [[Brady v. Maryland]], which concerns prosecutorial disclosure obligations) is a cornerstone case in guilty-plea jurisprudence. It established the totality-of-the-circumstances test for plea voluntariness and confirmed that defendants bear significant risk when entering pleas, even if the legal landscape later shifts in their favor.
